# Audience Research Protocol

Use this protocol only for audience research and topic generation.

## 1. Establish the market relationship

Translate the user's input into creator position, target audience, desired audience outcome, relationship, and exclusions. Treat the creator and audience as separate unless the user explicitly says the content is peer-to-peer or creator education.

Use the audience's original wording in the visible confirmation. Add a narrower label only when it improves clarity; never replace their wording with industry jargon.

When only a profession or business category is provided, infer the ordinary customer-facing audience and state the assumption. Examples:

- Real estate agent → local home buyers, sellers, and homeowners.
- Financial advisor → people making financial decisions, within the advisor's stated market and compliance limits.
- Wedding photographer → engaged couples planning a wedding.
- Affiliate marketer teaching beginners → beginners trying to start or improve affiliate marketing.

These are interpretation examples, not fixed mappings. The user's offer and stated audience control.

## 2. Search before ideating

Use the current session's native web-search capability before generating the ten pain points or twenty topics. Research the end audience, not merely the creator's industry.

Use multiple query angles:

- Questions the audience asks before buying or taking action.
- Problems, objections, misconceptions, risks, and costly mistakes.
- Recent changes affecting the audience's decisions.
- Desired outcomes and emotionally significant moments.
- Language used in public discussions, reviews, FAQs, forums, search results, and reputable industry sources.

Prefer evidence published or updated within the past 12 months when the topic changes quickly. Older sources may support stable, recurring problems, but do not label a subject “trending” solely because it appears in old material.

Open and inspect the sources used. Gather at least five relevant sources when available, including at least two sources that reflect audience questions or firsthand language. Do not treat five near-duplicate SEO articles as independent evidence.

Avoid research dominated by advice about posting, algorithms, follower growth, or becoming a content creator unless the actual target audience is creators. A source is relevant only when it reveals something about the end audience's decisions, frustrations, risks, or goals.

## 3. Report research status honestly

Use one of these labels:

- **Live research completed:** sufficient current public evidence was inspected.
- **Partial live research:** some relevant evidence was found, but coverage was incomplete. Identify which conclusions include inference.
- **Inference-only:** live search was unavailable or produced no usable evidence.

If search is unavailable, say:

> Live web search is not available in this chat. I can create an inference-only topic set from established audience patterns, but I will not present it as current research. Would you like me to continue?

Wait for the user's permission before producing an inference-only set. Never use the phrase “Current public suggestions were unavailable,” and never hide missing research behind generic copy.

## 4. Synthesize ten pain points

Rank pain points using these signals:

1. Frequency across independent sources.
2. Urgency or consequence for the audience.
3. Emotional intensity or decision friction.
4. Relevance to the creator's credible expertise or offer.
5. Suitability for a useful short-form explanation.

Present a compact table:

| # | Audience pain point | Evidence signal | Why it matters now |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |

Write each pain point in natural language that the audience would recognize. Do not manufacture numerical rankings, search volume, or trend percentages.

## 5. Generate twenty audience-facing topics

Create two distinct topics for each pain point. Vary the value angle across diagnosis, mistake, myth, decision guide, demonstration, checklist, story, warning, comparison, and next step.

Present:

| # | Potential video topic | Pain point served | Audience payoff |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |

Every topic must:

- Address the target audience directly.
- Promise one clear and honest benefit.
- Be narrow enough for one short-form video.
- Avoid unexplained jargon and generic filler.
- Fit the creator's position without pretending to know their personal story.
- Differ materially from the other nineteen topics.

## 6. Cite the evidence

End with a compact source list containing descriptive source titles and direct links. Do not cite search-result pages. Place a citation near any time-sensitive or factual claim that depends on it.

Then invite the user to reply with a topic number or paste a topic into a rough outline.

## Research quality test

Reject and revise the topic set if any answer is yes:

- Would these topics make equal sense for nearly any industry?
- Are the topics teaching the creator how to make content instead of helping the end audience?
- Did the wording replace the user's specific audience with a broad niche label?
- Are “trending” claims unsupported by current sources?
- Are multiple topics merely paraphrases of the same idea?
